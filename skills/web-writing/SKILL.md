# web-writing

Most people do not read web pages. They scan them. In Nielsen's study 79% of users scanned every new page and only 16% read word by word. Write for the scanner first; the rare full reader is served too.

Source: Jakob Nielsen, "How Users Read on the Web", Nielsen Norman Group, https://www.nngroup.com/articles/how-users-read-on-the-web/

## What the study measured

Five versions of the same site, same facts, different writing. Usability (task time, errors, memory, sense of site structure) compared with a promotional control version:

| Version | Change | Better by |
|---|---|---|
| Concise | about half the words | 58% |
| Scannable | same words, laid out as a list | 47% |
| Objective | no boasting or hype | 27% |
| All three combined | concise + scannable + objective | 124% |

Hype costs more than taste: readers spend effort filtering out exaggeration, and their first reaction to a boastful claim is "no, it isn't", which slows them down.

## Rules

1. **Conclusion first (inverted pyramid).** The first sentence says the answer, the verdict or the one thing the reader does. Background comes after, if at all.
2. **Half the words.** Cut to about half of a normal draft. Delete warm-up sentences, restated points and filler.
3. **One idea per paragraph.** Readers judge a paragraph by its first few words and skip the rest, so a second idea in the same paragraph is lost.
4. **Highlight keywords.** Bold the words that carry the meaning, so a scanner reading only the bold gets the message. Links count as highlighting too; do not bold everything.
5. **Plain subheadings, not clever ones.** A heading says what the section contains ("Pricing", "How to cancel"), never a pun or teaser.
6. **Bulleted lists** for any series of three or more items, numbers or steps.
7. **Objective language.** State facts, numbers and specifics. No "best ever", "revolutionary", "world-class", "seamless". If a claim needs an adjective, replace it with the evidence.
8. **Earn credibility.** Say who is behind the claim, show real numbers, and link out to sources. Outbound links signal the homework was done.

## How to apply it

When asked to write or rewrite:

1. Find the one point. Write it as the first sentence.
2. Draft, then cut the draft to roughly half.
3. Split paragraphs so each holds one idea; turn any series into a list.
4. Bold 1 to 3 keywords per section. Replace headings that are clever with headings that are literal.
5. Hunt for marketese: every boastful adjective becomes a fact or is deleted.
6. Check: reading only the headings, the bold words and the first line of each paragraph still tells the whole story. If not, fix it.

## Example

Before (promotional, 41 words):
> Our amazing new dashboard is the most powerful way ever built to stay on top of your team's work, packed with incredible features that will completely transform how you manage projects, track progress and keep everyone aligned every single day.

After (concise, scannable, objective, 24 words):
> **The dashboard shows your team's work on one screen:**
> - open tasks per person
> - what is overdue
> - what changed since yesterday

## Output

Return the rewritten text only, unless the user asks for an explanation. If asked to review instead of rewrite, list the rule each problem breaks, worst first, with the fixed line next to it.
