# Plain English after Zinsser and Orwell

William Zinsser (1922-2015), journalist and Yale writing teacher, wrote "On Writing Well". His core idea matches Wolf Schneider's in German: writing is a service to the reader, and clutter is the enemy. George Orwell's six rules from "Politics and the English Language" (1946) add the discipline. Every rule below follows from that.

Apply these principles to all English text you write or edit: articles, newsletters, ads, emails, reports, web copy. The rules are guidelines, not laws. As Orwell's last rule says, break any of them sooner than write something barbarous, but never to sound clever.

## Workflow

**Writing new text**
1. Before drafting, state the core message in one sentence. If you can't, clarify the point first. Zinsser: clear thinking becomes clear writing; one can't exist without the other.
2. Draft for the reader: who are they, what do they already know, what do they need?
3. Revise against the checklist at the end. Cut first, then sharpen.

**Editing existing text**
1. Read the whole text once for its message.
2. Revise sentence by sentence using the rules below.
3. Keep the author's meaning, facts and voice. Don't add claims.
4. If the user wants to learn, briefly list the main types of changes (e.g. "clutter cut", "passive made active", "nouns turned into verbs") with one or two examples each. Otherwise just deliver the revised text.

## 1. Attitude

- **The reader comes first.** The reader has a short attention span and owes you nothing. Every sentence must earn its place.
- **Clarity before elegance.** Writing that is hard to follow is not deep, it is badly made.
- **No showing off.** Don't write to impress colleagues, bosses or experts. Write so an interested outsider understands.
- **Think first, then write.** Muddy text usually comes from muddy thinking.
- **Be yourself.** Write as a person talking to a person. Use "I" and "you" when it fits.

## 2. Words

- **Short, common words** over long or rare ones (Orwell rule 2): "use" not "utilize", "help" not "facilitate", "start" not "commence", "about" not "with regard to".
- **Concrete, not abstract.** Words the reader can picture: "rain" not "precipitation event", "chairs, desks, lamps" not "furnishings".
- **Verbs are the strongest words.** Turn nouns made from verbs back into verbs (watch for -tion, -ment, -ance, -ence, -ity, -ize).
  - ✗ "We conducted an investigation of the incident" → ✓ "We investigated the incident"
- **No weak verb plus noun pairs.**
  - ✗ "make a decision" → ✓ "decide"
  - ✗ "give consideration to" → ✓ "consider"
  - ✗ "carry out an analysis" → ✓ "analyze"
- **Few adjectives and adverbs.** Cut those that add nothing or repeat the noun or verb: "past history", "end result", "free gift", "completely unanimous", "smile happily". Keep those that distinguish.
- **Cut clutter (Zinsser).** "at this point in time" → "now"; "in order to" → "to"; "due to the fact that" → "because"; "in the event that" → "if"; "it is important to note that" → delete.
- **Cut qualifiers:** very, really, quite, rather, somewhat, a bit, sort of, kind of, basically, actually, literally (unless literal).
- **No stale figures of speech** (Orwell rule 1): never use a metaphor or phrase you are used to seeing in print. "Level playing field", "move the needle", "low-hanging fruit", "at the end of the day", "think outside the box".
- **No inflated words or buzzwords:** "leverage", "synergy", "robust", "seamless", "cutting-edge", "best-in-class", "game-changer", "going forward", "touch base", "circle back".
- **Same thing, same word.** Don't swap synonyms for variety. If you mean the user, say "user" every time, not "user ... customer ... client ... member".
- **Jargon out, or explained** (Orwell rule 5): no foreign phrase, scientific word or jargon if there is an everyday English equivalent. Same for euphemism: "fired" not "let go", "price rise" not "price adjustment".
- **Avoid abbreviations** unless everyone knows them (US, EU). Spell the word out.
- **Few superlatives.** If everything is "unique" and "revolutionary", nothing is.

## 3. Sentences

- **Short sentences.** Most sentences should be well under 20 words. Long sentences are fine when they run in a straight line, but keep them the exception. Vary length for rhythm.
- **Main point in the main clause.** Side information goes in subordinate clauses or a new sentence.
  - ✗ "The minister, who had, after returning from Brussels late on Monday, met the press, announced his resignation."
  - ✓ "The minister resigned. He announced it on Monday, soon after returning from Brussels."
- **No nested clauses.** Don't break a sentence open with inserted clauses. Split it.
- **Subject and verb early and close together.** The reader should know fast who does what.
- **Active, not passive** (Orwell rule 4). Passive hides the actor. ✗ "It was decided" → ✓ "The board decided". Use passive only when the actor truly doesn't matter.
- **No noun stacks.** ✗ "customer onboarding process improvement initiative" → ✓ "a plan to make it easier for new customers to get started".
- **No chains of "of".** ✗ "the results of the review of the causes of the outage".
- **Positive, not negative.** "He was late" not "He was not on time". Avoid double negatives entirely.
- **One idea per sentence.**
- **Numbers sparingly and vividly.** Few numbers per sentence. Round where precision doesn't matter. Make big numbers graspable ("the size of two football fields").
- **Cut every word you can** (Orwell rule 3): if a word can be cut, cut it.

## 4. Text and structure

- **The first sentence decides.** Start with something concrete: a fact, a scene, a person, the news. No throat-clearing ("In today's fast-paced world ...", "As we all know ...").
- **Most important first** for news and functional text (emails, reports). The reader may stop after any paragraph.
- **One topic per paragraph.** Short paragraphs, clear transitions.
- **Show, don't assert.** Examples, people, scenes, comparisons.
- **A strong ending.** Stop when you're done. End on a point, an image or a consequence, not a weak summary or "only time will tell".
- **Cutting is half the work.** Almost every first draft improves by cutting 10 to 30%. Zinsser: most first drafts can be cut by half without losing anything.
- **Read it aloud.** If you stumble or run out of breath, the sentence needs work.
- **Use punctuation on purpose.** Colons for clarity. Exclamation marks rarely. No scare quotes as a substitute for saying what you mean.

## Checklist (before handing in)

- [ ] Can the core message be said in one sentence, and does the text deliver it?
- [ ] Does the first sentence pull the reader in?
- [ ] Any sentence over about 25 words? Split or simplify it.
- [ ] Any nested clauses, or a subject far from its verb?
- [ ] Is the main point in the main clause?
- [ ] Nouns made from verbs turned back into verbs where possible?
- [ ] Weak verb plus noun pairs replaced (make a decision → decide)?
- [ ] Passive turned active where the actor matters?
- [ ] Clutter, qualifiers, buzzwords and stale phrases removed?
- [ ] Unneeded adjectives, adverbs and redundancies removed?
- [ ] Same thing called by the same word?
- [ ] Jargon, euphemisms and abbreviations replaced or explained?
- [ ] Concrete words and examples instead of abstractions?
- [ ] Does the ending land?
- [ ] Read aloud: does it flow?

## Limits

- Keep the register the context needs. A contract or a scientific paper may need precise technical terms; still apply the sentence rules.
- Don't flatten a deliberate author voice into uniform short sentences. Rhythm comes from variation.
- When editing someone else's text, never change facts, quotes or meaning for the sake of style.
- For German text, use the schneider-deutsch-stil skill instead: it adds German-specific rules (Satzklammer, Nominalstil suffixes) this one lacks.
